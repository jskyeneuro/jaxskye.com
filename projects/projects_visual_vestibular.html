Research Project

Visual-Vestibular Conflict Detection

How active head movement and fixation type modulate the brain's ability to detect mismatches between visual and vestibular signals

Aims

Stable perception of a visual environment depends on the brain's ability to compare and evaluate the congruence of visual and vestibular signals. When these signals mismatch as in simulator sickness, vertigo, or motion sickness the consequences can be significant. Prior work suggested that sensitivity to visual-vestibular conflict is modulated by fixation type, with eye movement signals playing an important role. This research project investigated whether active head movement signals, specifically neck motor commands and proprioception, also modulate conflict detection, and whether the type of visual fixation (head-fixed vs. scene-fixed) interacts with this.

  • Determine whether active head movement improves visual-vestibular conflict detection relative to passive head movement
  • Test whether scene-fixed fixation enhances conflict detection compared to head-fixed fixation
  • Assess the interaction between head movement type and fixation condition on conflict sensitivity (PSE) and precision (JND)

Description & Methods

16 healthy adult participants completed a two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) task across 4 blocks of 302 trials each. Participants were seated in a rotating chair and presented with a starfield stimulus via an HTC Vive Pro head-mounted display. On each trial, head movement occurred either actively (self-generated) or passively (externally driven), and visual fixation was either head-fixed (fixation point moved with the head) or scene-fixed (fixation point remained stationary in the world). Visual gain — the ratio of visual to vestibular motion — was varied between 0.7 and 1.3. Participants judged whether the visual scene moved "with" or "against" the direction of head motion, allowing estimation of the point of subjective equality (PSE) and just-noticeable difference (JND) for each condition via psychometric curve fitting. Head position was tracked using OptiTrack Prime 13 infrared motion capture and eye movements were recorded using a Pupil Labs eye-tracking add-on.

Results showed that active head movement with scene-fixed fixation produced the best conflict detection, with a significant effect of active vs. passive movement on PSE (F(1,15) = 11.15, p = 0.045) and a trend toward significance for JND (F(1,15) = 3.851, p = 0.069). Scene-fixed fixation yielded a significant effect on JND compared to head-fixed fixation (F(1,15) = 5.552, p = 0.033). These findings suggest that conflict detection is modulated by motor commands including both eye and head movement signals, with scene-fixed fixation potentially reducing retinal image motion velocity and increasing precision in head movement estimation.
