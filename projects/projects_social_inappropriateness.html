Research Project

Brain Lesions & Theory of Mind Deficits

Multimodal lesion-deficit mapping of the brain networks supporting socially appropriate behavior

Aims

Behaving appropriately within social contexts requires the dynamic integration of others' beliefs, intentions, and desires, a capacity broadly referred to as mentalizing or theory of mind. Focal brain lesions offer a unique window into the causal neurobiology of this capacity, but prior work has largely relied on individual case studies or has not characterized how lesion location disrupts the broader functional and structural networks underlying social behavior. This project applied a multimodal lesion-deficit approach to identify not just where lesions cause social behavior change, but which functional and structural networks those lesion sites are embedded in.

  • Identify brain lesion locations associated with increased socially inappropriate behavior following focal brain damage using lesion-symptom mapping
  • Characterize the functional network connected to those lesion sites using normative resting-state fMRI data, and test its overlap with the canonical mentalizing network
  • Identify structural white matter pathways connected to lesion sites associated with social behavior change using normative diffusion MRI tractography
  • Assess overlap between lesion-derived functional networks and canonical resting-state networks, including the Default Mode and Limbic networks

Description & Methods

155 participants (66 female; mean age 54.9 years) from the Iowa Neurological Patient Registry who sustained a first-time brain lesion after age 18 were included. Social behavior was measured via the Social Inappropriateness (SI) scale of the Iowa Scales of Personality Change (ISPC), a 30-item informant-report measure assessing behavioral change before versus after lesion onset — completed by a close informant who knew the participant well both before and after the lesion. Lesions were manually traced on native T1-weighted images and transformed to MNI space using enantiomorphic normalization for unilateral lesions and cost-function masking for bilateral lesions. Mass univariate lesion-symptom mapping with permutation testing (10,000 permutations) and family-wise error correction identified lesion locations associated with SI change, controlling for lesion volume.

Functional lesion network mapping (fLNM) used normative resting-state fMRI data from 1,000 healthy adults (Genome Superstruct Project) to generate functional connectivity maps seeded at each participant's lesion. Structural lesion network mapping (sLNM) used normative diffusion MRI tractography from the Human Connectome Project (N=1,200) to identify white matter tracts connected to each lesion. Overlap between the fLNM-derived SI network and canonical resting-state networks (Yeo 7-network parcellation) and a Neurosynth-derived mentalizing network (151 studies) was assessed using spatial correlation and Dice coefficients.

Lesion-symptom mapping implicated medial frontal regions including the gyrus rectus, frontal pole, medial and orbital frontal cortex, and anterior cingulate bilaterally. The fLNM-derived SI network encompassed medial frontal cortex, precuneus, hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, cerebellar lobule IX, right temporoparietal junction (TPJ), and bilateral anterior temporal lobe — overlapping significantly with the Default Mode Network, Limbic Network, and the Neurosynth mentalizing network. Structural LNM highlighted frontal white matter and the genu of the corpus callosum (forceps minor), structurally connecting the implicated medial frontal regions across hemispheres.
