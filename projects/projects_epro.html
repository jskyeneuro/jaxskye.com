Research Project

Low-Level Acoustic Feature Perception in Emotional Speech

Emotional prosodic context modulates sensitivity to pitch and loudness; individual differences vary with subclinical psychopathology traits

Aims

Speech carries meaning by the words spoken and the emotional tone in which they are delivered. Emotional prosody, or the way pitch, tempo, and loudness are varied to convey feeling, is a core channel of social comunication. Decades of research have examined how well people recognize emotional prosody, but a more basic question has gone largely unasked: Does the emotional content of speech change how the auditory system processes the acoustic signal itself?

  • Identify differences in pitch and loudness perception across three emotional states of the speaker: happy, angry, and neutral
  • Explore the relationship between pitch and loudness sensitivity and socially relevant psychopathology traits in a nonclinical population

Description & Main Findings

Neurotypical adults listened to semantically neutral clips spoken with angry, happy, or neutral prosody and judged which one was altered in pitch or loudness. Two control conditions removed prosodic structure of the speech clips were administered to independent samples to isolate whether observed effects were due to prosodic features or were purely acoustic.

Emotional context significantly modulated perception of both features, but in different ways. Loudness differences were detected less accurately than angry or neutral speech, an effect that disappeared in the control condition, confirming it depended on intact prosodic structure. For pitch, a different pattern emerged. The highest accuracy was in the neutral context and this neutral advantage was absent in the control condition, again implicating prosodic structure in the observed effect. Notably, intact prosodic speech produced substantially higher overall psychoacoustic sensitivity than either control condition.

Higher autistic traits predicted a smaller loudness advantage for neutral over happy speech, while higher social anxiety predicted a smaller pitch advantage for neutral over both emotional conditions.
