Research Project

Low-Level Acoustic Feature Perception in Emotional Speech

Emotional prosodic context modulates sensitivity to pitch and loudness — and individual differences vary with subclinical psychopathology traits

Aims

Social communication relies not only on the words we speak but on how we speak them. Emotional prosody, the modulation of pitch, tempo, loudness, and other acoustic features to convey emotional state, is a fundamental channel of social communication. Prior work has shown that psychoacoustic abilities predict emotional prosody recognition, and that individuals with conditions like autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and social anxiety show differences in prosody perception. However, most paradigms treat acoustic feature perception and emotional prosody recognition as separate processes. A critical gap remains: can emotional context itself feed back to modulate sensitivity to low-level acoustic features? This study tested this question directly using a psychoacoustic oddball paradigm in which acoustic modulations were embedded within emotionally prosodic speech.

  • Aim 1: Determine whether emotional prosodic context (angry, happy, neutral) modulates sensitivity to low-level acoustic feature changes (specifically pitch and loudness) using accuracy and reaction time as dependent measures
  • Aim 2: Evaluate whether individual differences in emotion-modulated acoustic feature perception are associated with subclinical psychopathology traits, including autistic traits (AQ, SRS) and social anxiety (LSAS)

Description & Methods

Neurotypical young adults (n = 50 main analysis; n = 53 quilted control; n = 59 shuffled control) completed a three-interval forced-choice oddball paradigm. On each trial, participants heard three sequential speech clips — semantically neutral utterances spoken with angry, happy, or neutral prosody — and identified which of the first and third clips differed from the middle (target) clip. Acoustic modulations of either pitch or loudness (3.5% change) were applied to one of the flanking clips. Emotional context was interleaved randomly across trials. Participants also completed three self-report psychopathology measures between task blocks: the Autism Quotient (AQ), Social Responsiveness Scale–II (SRS), and Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale (LSAS). Two control conditions — quilted speech (local spectrotemporal statistics preserved, long-term prosodic structure removed) and shuffled speech (all temporal structure destroyed) — were administered to independent samples to isolate prosodic contributions to the observed effects. Repeated-measures ANOVAs with emotional context as a within-subjects factor were used for the main and control analyses; linear models evaluated the relationship between emotion difference scores and psychopathology traits.

For loudness, emotional context significantly modulated accuracy (F(2,98) = 5.674, p = .005, η²p = .104): happy prosody was identified less accurately than both angry (p = .003, d = .44) and neutral (p = .025, d = .36). For pitch, neutral prosody yielded significantly higher accuracy than both angry (p = .012, d = .46) and happy (p = .003, d = .57) contexts (F(2,96) = 7.429, p = .001, η²p = .134). Control analyses supported a prosodic rather than purely acoustic explanation: the loudness emotion effect was not replicated in shuffled speech, and the pitch effect pattern differed between normal and control conditions. Intact prosodic structure also enhanced overall psychoacoustic sensitivity relative to both control conditions. For Aim 2, AQ and SRS total scores were associated with emotion-related differences in loudness perception, while LSAS total scores were associated with emotion-related differences in pitch perception.
