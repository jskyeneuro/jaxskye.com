Research Project

Geometry of Post-Stroke Behavior

Uncovering the geometry of post-stroke behavioral impairment and predicting long-term recovery

Aims

Stroke produces a wide range of behavioral deficits whose trajectories vary substantially across patients. A central but underexplored question is: what is the shape of stroke-induced behavioral impairment across a patient population, and what does this structure reveal about the relationship between brain structure and function? Prior work showed that behavioral deficits after stroke are highly variable, strongly influenced by lesion size and location, and change dramatically during recovery, yet some recovery trajectories are more predictable than others. Standard linear approaches to dimensionality reduction (e.g., PCA) may miss the nonlinear structure that organizes patients in high-dimensional behavior space.

  • Characterize the high-dimensional geometry of post-stroke behavioral profiles using nonlinear dimensionality reduction (UMAP), and determine whether patients are distributed along a continuous low-dimensional manifold
  • Identify behavioral archetypes (characteristic profiles that serve as landmarks across this manifold) and test whether they correlate with lesion location
  • Benchmark the predictive power of nonlinear embeddings against linear alternatives (PCA, Factor Analysis, K-Means, hierarchical clustering) for predicting chronic functional, motor, and cognitive outcomes
  • Identify a minimal set of subacute behavioral measures that predict chronic outcomes as accurately as the full test battery

Description & Methods

96 adults who sustained a first-time unilateral ischemic stroke were assessed 7–14 days post-stroke (subacute epoch) on 78 behavioral measures spanning motor function, language, visuospatial attention, and memory. Chronic functional, motor, and cognitive outcomes were assessed at 3 months post-stroke. Five dimensionality reduction techniques (UMAP, PCA, Factor Analysis, K-Means clustering, and hierarchical agglomerative clustering) were applied to the subacute behavioral data to extract low-dimensional embeddings. Ridge regression with 50-repeat 80/20 cross-validation was used to predict 188 chronic outcomes from each embedding, with scrambled-data comparisons to guard against overfitting. Prediction accuracy distributions were compared across embeddings using the Mann-Whitney U-test. Structural MRI lesion data from 75 patients were used in multivariate lesion-symptom mapping to identify brain regions associated with behavioral profiles derived from the UMAP embedding.

UMAP embeddings outperformed all other dimensionality reduction approaches, yielding functional outcome predictions statistically equivalent to those from all 78 predictors — across multiple embedding dimensionalities. The 2D UMAP embedding revealed a continuous behavioral manifold with no strong clusters, which was summarized using 8 K-Means-derived behavioral archetypes. Four of these archetypes localized significantly to distinct brain regions, including lateralized motor profiles associating with contralateral hemisphere damage and a language-deficit profile linking to left posterior deep white matter and the superior longitudinal fasciculus. Finally, an iterative procedure identified two subacute measures — Combined Walking Index and BVMT 25-minute delayed recall — whose combination predicted functional outcomes as well as all 78 predictors. Adding two fine motor measures (right ARAT pinch item; left 9-Hole Grooved Pegboard) extended comparable prediction accuracy to motor outcomes as well.
